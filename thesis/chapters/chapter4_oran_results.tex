\chapter{Simulation Results}
\label{chap:oran-results}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:oran-experimental-setup}

All results in this chapter use the environment, power model, and BMPP-DQN
architecture specified in Chapter~\ref{chap:oran-system-model}, instantiated
at this track's single focused scenario: $R=4$ RUs, $U=8$ UEs, one DU, one
CU, and $S=3$ functional-split options
(\texttt{config/oran\_default.yaml}). BMPP-DQN is compared against three
baselines -- a purely discrete method (DQN, thresholding the continuous
actions), a purely continuous method (DDPG, relaxing the discrete actions),
and an existing parameterized-action method (MP-DQN, processing the full
joint discrete-continuous action space in a single flat network, unlike
BMPP-DQN's per-branch decomposition) -- under matched training and
evaluation conditions: $500$ training episodes of $100$ environment steps
each, identical traffic and channel realizations per seed, and the
hyperparameters listed in Section~\ref{sec:oran-bmpp-training}'s Default
Hyperparameters paragraph. Training used GPU-accelerated PyTorch on
RunPod-hosted A40/RTX~4090 instances.

The canonical protocol evaluates every method over $n=3$ random seeds
($42$, $123$, $456$), per Concept Note Section 5.3 and this thesis's own
Quality Gate 5 as originally scoped. $n=3$ is a thin basis for statistical
power; Section~\ref{sec:oran-n10} reports a supplementary $n=10$-seed
validation of the same four methods that was run specifically to check
whether the $n=3$ findings below hold up with more statistical power. It
does not replace the canonical $n=3$ comparison, which remains this
chapter's primary result.

Five operational metrics are reported for every method: mean episodic
reward (Eq.~\ref{eq:oran-reward}), mean total power $P_{\mathrm{total}}$ in
watts, two distinct QoS satisfaction rates, switching frequency
$n_{\mathrm{sw}}(t)$ averaged per step, and aggregate downlink throughput in
Mbps. The two QoS rates require separate definitions because they differ
sharply in practice: \emph{QoS Rate} (``QoS Strict'' in the multi-criteria
tables) is the fraction of evaluation steps at which \emph{every} UE is
simultaneously satisfied ($\max_u \max(0, D_u(t)-C_u(t)) = 0$ for all $u$);
\emph{Per-UE QoS} is the mean, per step, of the \emph{fraction} of UEs that
are individually satisfied. Because the strict criterion requires $U=8$
roughly-independent per-UE satisfaction events to co-occur, it falls off far
more sharply than the per-UE rate even when most individual UEs are usually
satisfied -- this is why every method in Table~\ref{tab:oran-convergence}
below shows a QoS Rate in the 30-55\% range alongside a Per-UE QoS above
80\%, rather than the two tracking each other closely. Both are reported
because they answer different questions: Per-UE QoS reflects aggregate
service quality across the UE population, while QoS Rate reflects how often
the network is in a state where no UE at all is under-served.

Statistical comparisons against BMPP-DQN use a paired $t$-test (paired on
seed) and Cohen's $d$ for effect size, following this thesis's own Quality
Gate 5 convention.

BMPP-DQN's training reward is highly variable in early episodes --
reflecting both $\epsilon$-greedy exploration at its initial value

both the upper- and lower-level updates -- and stabilizes as exploration and
continuous noise decay toward their end values. By the final checkpoint
(episode 500), BMPP-DQN's evaluation performance sits between DQN and MP-DQN
on reward, consistent with Table~\ref{tab:oran-convergence}
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-performance-comparison}).

Table~\ref{tab:oran-convergence} and Figures~\ref{fig:oran-comparison-1}--\ref{fig:oran-pareto}
summarize the canonical $n=3$ comparison. Three observations stand out.

First, \textbf{no pairwise reward comparison against BMPP-DQN reaches
conventional statistical significance at $n=3$}: $p=0.904$ against DQN,
$p=0.179$ against DDPG, $p=0.093$ against MP-DQN. MP-DQN's reward is the
best of the four methods (least negative, $-15452.55$), with a large
negative Cohen's $d$ ($-1.760$) indicating a substantial effect size that
nonetheless falls short of significance given only 3 seeds -- precisely the
thin-statistical-power concern this thesis's supplementary $n=10$ check
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-n10}) was designed to address.

Second, \textbf{BMPP-DQN and MP-DQN post essentially tied mean power}
($162.5\,\mathrm{W}$ vs.\ $160.2\,\mathrm{W}$), both well below DQN
($213.4\,\mathrm{W}$) and DDPG ($189.5\,\mathrm{W}$). BMPP-DQN does
\emph{not} undercut MP-DQN on power, which matters directly for Objective 4
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-objectives}): a $\geq15\%$ energy-saving target is
not met against the strongest baseline on this metric alone, a point
revisited without qualification in
Section~\ref{sec:oran-rq-answers} and Chapter~\ref{chap:oran-conclusion}.

Third, \textbf{switching frequency separates the methods sharply}. DDPG's
near-zero switching ($0.04$ events/step) follows directly from its purely
continuous relaxation, which never flips a discrete RU/split decision at
all in the usual sense. Of the three methods that do make discrete
decisions, BMPP-DQN's independent per-branch exploration and two-timescale
cadence (Section~\ref{sec:oran-bmpp-training}) yields the lowest switching
frequency ($0.20$), well below DQN ($0.29$) and more than $5\times$ below
MP-DQN ($1.04$) -- MP-DQN's best-in-class reward comes with the least
stable discrete-action behavior of the three methods that actually activate
and deactivate RUs. This switching-stability advantage, not a raw
energy-savings win, is the pattern that motivates the multi-criteria
analysis in the next section.

\section{Multi-Criteria Composite Analysis}
\label{sec:oran-mcda}

Reward already aggregates energy efficiency, QoS shortfall, and switching
cost into a single training objective
(Eq.~\ref{eq:oran-reward}), which makes it unsuitable as an independent
cross-check of whether that combination is actually well-balanced across
methods. This section instead applies TOPSIS (Technique for Order
Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution)~\cite{hwang1981topsis} with
equal criterion weights, directly to the five raw operational metrics
(power, QoS Strict, QoS Per-UE, switching frequency, throughput) --
deliberately excluding reward itself, per the module's own docstring.

\input{../tables_oran/multicriteria_summary_oran.tex}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{../figures_oran/composite_score_oran.pdf}
\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{../figures_oran/radar_comparison_oran.pdf}
\caption{TOPSIS composite score (left) and normalized multi-criteria radar
  comparison (right) across all 4 methods.}
\label{fig:oran-mcda}
\end{figure}

Under this independent, equal-weighted cross-check, BMPP-DQN ranks
\textbf{first} (TOPSIS score $0.833$), ahead of DDPG ($0.651$), DQN
($0.640$), and MP-DQN ($0.356$ -- last, despite its best-in-class reward
and power, driven down by its substantially worse switching frequency).
This is the first piece of evidence that BMPP-DQN's contribution is better
characterized as a balanced multi-criteria trade-off than as a raw
energy-saving win on any single metric.

\section{Robustness of the Multi-Criteria Ranking}
\label{sec:oran-mcda-robustness}

A single MCDA method and weighting scheme could make Section~\ref{sec:oran-mcda}'s
ranking an artifact of that specific choice. To check this, the same five
metrics are also ranked under entropy-weighted TOPSIS (objective,
data-driven weights computed from the metrics' own dispersion, rather than
equal weights)~\cite{zeleny1982mcdm} and under VIKOR, a different
aggregation rule based on weighted-sum group utility $S$ and
worst-single-criterion regret $R$ ($v=0.5$) rather than TOPSIS's
distance-to-ideal-solution geometry~\cite{opricovic2004vikor}.

\input{../tables_oran/mcda_robustness_oran.tex}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../figures_oran/mcda_robustness_oran.pdf}
\caption{MCDA ranking-robustness bump chart: each method's rank under
  equal-weighted TOPSIS, entropy-weighted TOPSIS, and entropy-weighted
  VIKOR.}
\label{fig:oran-mcda-robustness-fig}
\end{figure}

At $n=3$, BMPP-DQN is the \emph{only} method ranked first under all three
schemes simultaneously (Table~\ref{tab:oran-mcda-robustness}), with a VIKOR
regret score of $Q=0.000$ (the best attainable). DDPG, DQN, and MP-DQN all
change rank across at least one scheme. This agreement across three
independently-reasoned aggregation rules is stronger evidence for a
genuinely balanced trade-off than Section~\ref{sec:oran-mcda}'s
single-scheme result alone -- though Section~\ref{sec:oran-n10} reports that
this specific three-way agreement is not fully robust at $n=10$, an honest
caveat on an otherwise favorable finding.

\section{Power-Model Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:oran-sensitivity}

Every RU/DU/CU/fronthaul power constant in the model
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-power}) is a literature-informed but unvalidated
placeholder (Chapter~\ref{chap:oran-system-model}'s own needs-validation
disclosures). Concept Note Section 6.9 addresses whether the comparative
findings above depend on these placeholders' exact values by independently
scaling each of the four constant groups by $10\times$ and $0.1\times$ (8
perturbed configurations total, all others held at their default values)
and re-running the full 4-method/3-seed/500-episode protocol at each.

Table~\ref{tab:oran-sensitivity} shows that BMPP-DQN's TOPSIS rank is

strongest on the multi-criteria composite -- therefore does not depend on

resolves Concept Note Section 6.3's own long-standing ``[if conducted]''
sensitivity-analysis placeholder; it does not, and cannot, resolve the
constants' own absolute values, which remain open exactly as described in

\section{RQ3: Effect of the Two-Timescale Design}
\label{sec:oran-rq3}

Research Question 3 and Objective 5 (Section~\ref{sec:oran-objectives}) ask
how explicitly separating slow (RU activation, split selection) and fast
(power, PRB allocation) decisions across two timescales affects learning
convergence and energy efficiency, relative to a single-timescale
treatment of the same action space. {\sloppy To answer this directly,
BMPP-DQN is retrained with \texttt{upper\_level\_period\_steps} collapsed
from its default value of $10$ to $1$
(\texttt{config/\allowbreak rq3\_single\_timescale.yaml}), degenerating the
two-timescale separation to a no-op -- every other hyperparameter, and the
full 3-seed/500-episode protocol, held fixed.\par}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../figures_oran/rq3_ablation_oran.pdf}
\caption{Reward and switching frequency: two-timescale (proposed, $N=10$)
  vs.\ single-timescale ($N=1$) BMPP-DQN, mean across 3 seeds.}
\label{fig:oran-rq3}
\end{figure}

Collapsing to a single timescale yields a mean reward of $-19742.17$
(seeds $-21397.08$, $-19684.07$, $-18145.36$) -- marginally \emph{better}
(less negative) than the two-timescale default's $-21373.18$ -- with mean
power ($165.3\,\mathrm{W}$), strict QoS rate ($49.9\%$), and per-UE QoS
rate ($90.6\%$) all within a few percent of the two-timescale default
($162.5\,\mathrm{W}$, $49.5\%$, $90.4\%$). The two configurations'
energy/QoS trade-offs are, in other words, essentially equivalent.

Switching frequency is where the two designs diverge sharply: the
single-timescale variant's mean switching frequency is $1.234$ events/step
(seeds $1.222$, $0.714$, $1.766$) -- more than \emph{six times} the
two-timescale default's $0.20$. Forcing every RU-activation and
split-selection decision to be re-evaluated every single environment step,
rather than held for $N=10$ steps at a time, produces substantially more
frequent discrete-action flips for essentially no reward, power, or QoS
benefit.

This directly answers RQ3: \textbf{the two-timescale design's measurable
benefit is decision stability, not raw energy or QoS performance.} The
slow/fast separation does not cost BMPP-DQN reward or energy efficiency
relative to a single-timescale treatment of the same action space, and it
substantially reduces how often RUs are switched on/off or re-split --
consistent with Non-RT RIC decisions being intended to operate on a
$1$--$10\,\mathrm{s}$ cadence rather than being re-decided every
$10$--$100\,\mathrm{ms}$ Near-RT RIC step
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-background}).

Consistent with this track's focused single-gNB scope

sizes (contrast the C-RAN track's $R=5$-to-$50$ scalability sweep). All

additional architectural components relative to the single-network
baselines -- two separate encoders, a branching critic, and a continuous
parameter network, evaluated together for one decision
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-bmpp-architecture}) -- do not change this comparison's
qualitative conclusion, since all four remain comfortably within a single
forward-pass budget on commodity hardware, well inside the $10$--$100\,\mathrm{ms}$
Near-RT RIC decision loop this design's lower level targets.

\section{Supplementary Validation at \texorpdfstring{$n=10$}{n=10} Seeds}
\label{sec:oran-n10}

Sections~\ref{sec:oran-performance-comparison}--\ref{sec:oran-mcda-robustness}
use the canonical $n=3$-seed protocol (Concept Note Section 5.3). Given
this thesis's own Quality Gate 5 ($\geq10$ seeds with confidence intervals
and Cohen's $d$), a supplementary check extends every method's seed set
from 3 to 10 (seeds $42$, $123$, $456$ plus 7 additional seeds,
\texttt{data/results\_oran\_10seed/}) and regenerates the same table/figure
suite into a parallel location
(\texttt{thesis/tables\_oran\_10seed/}), without overwriting the canonical
$n=3$ artifacts referenced above.

\input{../tables_oran_10seed/multicriteria_summary_oran.tex}

\input{../tables_oran_10seed/mcda_robustness_oran.tex}

Two findings change between $n=3$ and $n=10$, and are reported honestly
rather than suppressed in favor of the more favorable $n=3$ picture.

First, \textbf{DDPG's reward comparison against BMPP-DQN reaches
significance at $n=10$} ($p=0.0359$, $d=0.779$) where it did not at $n=3$
($p=0.179$), even though DDPG's mean reward is actually less negative at
$n=10$ ($-51207.12$) than at $n=3$ ($-68536.61$). The reason is variance,
not a worsened mean: DDPG's $n=3$ confidence interval is extremely wide
($[-152304.82, 15231.60]$), driven by one or more outlier seeds, while its
$n=10$ interval is far tighter ($[-76819.81, -25594.43]$) and excludes
zero. Adding seeds did not reveal DDPG performing worse on average; it
revealed that DDPG consistently underperforms BMPP-DQN on reward, which 3
seeds alone were too noisy to establish. MP-DQN's reward advantage over
BMPP-DQN, by contrast, remains short of significance at $n=10$
($p=0.0714$), though closer to the conventional threshold than at $n=3$
($p=0.093$).

Second, and more consequential for Section~\ref{sec:oran-mcda-robustness}'s
headline claim: \textbf{BMPP-DQN is no longer ranked first under every MCDA
scheme at $n=10$.} Under equal-weighted TOPSIS, DDPG overtakes BMPP-DQN for
rank 1 ($0.798$ vs.\ $0.766$) -- driven by DDPG's still-near-zero switching
frequency ($0.04$) outweighing its worse power and QoS once 10 seeds'
worth of data narrow the other methods' advantage on those metrics. BMPP-DQN
recovers rank 1 under entropy-weighted TOPSIS (which weights switching
frequency lower, at $0.14$, than the equal-weight scheme's $0.20$) but
falls to rank 2 under VIKOR. Only DQN is ranked identically (3rd) under all
three schemes at $n=10$, where at $n=3$ it was BMPP-DQN that achieved full
agreement. BMPP-DQN remains rank 1 or 2 under every scheme at both sample
sizes -- it is never the worst performer by this measure -- but
Section~\ref{sec:oran-mcda-robustness}'s strongest claim, full three-scheme
agreement, is partly an $n=3$-specific artifact rather than a fully
sample-size-robust finding. This nuance is carried forward, not
smoothed over, into Chapter~\ref{chap:oran-conclusion}'s discussion of
RQ2.

\section{Discussion: Answering the Research Questions}
\label{sec:oran-rq-answers}

\textbf{RQ1} (architectural integration, Section~\ref{sec:oran-research-questions}):
BMPP-DQN trains stably end-to-end as a single coupled network combining
branching discrete decomposition with multi-pass continuous parameter
processing (Chapter~\ref{chap:oran-system-model}), across all 3 (and,
supplementarily, all 10) random seeds, with no divergence or
training-instability failures observed. This is an architectural and
training-stability claim, evidenced by the convergence behavior in
Section~\ref{sec:oran-convergence} and the consistent final performance
across seeds in Tables~\ref{tab:oran-convergence} and
\ref{tab:oran-convergence-n10}.

\textbf{RQ2} (energy-saving performance): the evidence does not support a
single-metric energy-saving win. BMPP-DQN's mean power is essentially tied
with MP-DQN's (the best of the four methods on this metric;
Section~\ref{sec:oran-performance-comparison} reports no formal
significance test on power specifically) and its reward is not the best of
the four methods at either sample size. Objective 4's $\geq15\%$ energy-saving target against these
baselines is, without qualification, \textbf{not met} by any result in
this chapter. What the evidence does support is a genuine multi-criteria
balance: BMPP-DQN is the strongest or joint-strongest method under every
MCDA scheme tested, at both sample sizes
(Sections~\ref{sec:oran-mcda}--\ref{sec:oran-n10}), and this balance is
robust to every power-model placeholder constant's order-of-magnitude
uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:oran-sensitivity}). This reframing --
multi-criteria balance across power, QoS, and switching stability, rather
than a raw energy-saving margin -- is this thesis's actual, evidence-based
answer to RQ2, as previewed in Section~\ref{sec:oran-objectives} and
developed further in Chapter~\ref{chap:oran-conclusion}.

\textbf{RQ3} (two-timescale design): directly answered in
Section~\ref{sec:oran-rq3}. Collapsing the two-timescale separation to a
single timescale does not measurably change reward, power, or QoS, but
increases switching frequency more than sixfold. The two-timescale design's
contribution is decision stability at the discrete-action level, matched to
the Non-RT/Near-RT RIC timescale separation this problem is built on
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-background}), not an energy-efficiency gain in its
own right.

Chapter~\ref{chap:oran-conclusion} returns to these three answers alongside
this thesis's scope and limitations
(Section~\ref{sec:oran-scope}) to state its overall contribution and
directions for future work.
